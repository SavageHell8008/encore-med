# Information Architecture Research — Cognitive Categorization for EnconeMed

## Executive Summary
This document researches how humans *cognitively* categorize medical/health-related items — not how competitor websites happen to organize their catalogs (see `06-navigation-research.md` and separate competitor teardown docs for that). The central question: when EnconeMed groups its catalog into concepts like Equipment, Accessories, Care Essentials, Home ICU, Respiratory, Mobility, Recovery, and Patient Care, do those groupings match how a real person's mind sorts these objects, or do they match how a clinician or warehouse manager would sort them? Card-sorting methodology (Nielsen Norman Group) and Eleanor Rosch's prototype/basic-level category theory are the two anchor bodies of research used here. The key finding: category systems designed around internal/clinical logic reliably fail users who are searching under stress, and a "basic level" categorization (the level of abstraction humans reach for fastest) usually sits between an overly clinical label ("durable medical equipment," "DME," "ambulatory aids") and an overly specific product name ("Rollz Motion Rollator").

## Research Scope
- How card sorting reveals mental models distinct from internal/organizational taxonomies (NN/g).
- Rosch's prototype theory and basic-level categorization as a cognitive-science explanation for *why* certain groupings feel natural and others feel effortful.
- Application of these two bodies of research to the specific candidate IA labels EnconeMed is considering: Equipment, Accessories, Care Essentials, Home ICU, Respiratory, Mobility, Recovery, Patient Care.
- Explicit separation of "clinician categorization" vs. "stressed layperson categorization" as two distinct mental models that may need to coexist in the IA (e.g., through cross-linking or dual-path navigation) rather than a single taxonomy trying to serve both.
- Out of scope: navigation UI patterns (mega menu, breadcrumbs — see file 06), filter/facet design (see file 07), competitor site audits.

## Evidence
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Card sorting is an NN/g-documented UX research method in which participants group content items (typically written on cards) into categories that make sense to them and then label those categories; it is used specifically because "the core problem card sorting addresses is that designers think about content differently from users — internal teams organize by department or product line, while users organize by task, goal, or how they understand the world" (Nielsen Norman Group, "Card Sorting: Uncover Users' Mental Models," https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-definition/).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** NN/g's companion research on terminology notes that card-sort labels must be kept neutral, because participants otherwise default to keyword-matching rather than genuine conceptual grouping — implying that category *names* themselves (not just groupings) carry strong cognitive weight (Nielsen Norman Group, "Card Sorting: Pushing Users Beyond Terminology Matches," https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Eleanor Rosch's prototype theory (1971 onward) established that human categorization is graded rather than binary — category membership is judged by similarity to a prototype (the most representative example), not by satisfying a fixed checklist of defining features (multiple secondary sources summarizing Rosch, e.g. "Prototype Theory," Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_theory; "Rosch's Principles of Categorization," https://thereitis.org/principles-of-categorization/).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Rosch's theory identifies a "basic level" of categorization — the level of abstraction humans reach for by default because it maximizes shared attributes among members while minimizing cognitive effort (the "principle of cognitive economy"). Basic-level categories are described as having common attributes, common motor interactions, and similar overall shape/gestalt (source: summaries of Rosch's basic-level research, e.g. "Basic Level Categorisation and the Law," Springer, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11196-022-09928-z; "Basic Level Categorization Facilitates Visual Object Recognition," arXiv, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.04103).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** In basic-level terms, "wheelchair" is a basic-level category; "mobility aid" is a superordinate category (more abstract, fewer shared physical attributes); "manual transport wheelchair with swing-away footrests" is a subordinate category (more specific, harder to distinguish at a glance). Rosch's original research found people name and recognize objects fastest at the basic level, not the superordinate or subordinate level.
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Health-information-seeking research documents a persistent terminology gap between clinicians and laypeople: patients and caregivers frequently do not know or use clinical vocabulary, and this gap is a well-studied predictor of poor health-information retrieval outcomes (Zielstorff/consumer health vocabulary literature summarized in "Characteristics of Consumer Terminology for Health Information Retrieval," ResearchGate, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11041946; JMIR, "Predictors of Health Information-Seeking Behavior," https://www.jmir.org/2021/7/e21680). Age and education level are noted as the strongest predictors of health literacy and, by extension, of how "clinical" a caregiver's search vocabulary is likely to be.
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** A cross-sectional survey of laypeople's online health search strategies distinguishes "evidence-based" searching (symptom-detail-driven) from "hypothesis-based" searching (diagnosis-driven) — i.e., people search either from "what's happening to my father" or from "what he was just diagnosed with," rarely from equipment-category names (PMC, "Laypeople's Online Health Information Search Strategies," https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9482072/).

## Industry Research
- **[Industry Best Practice]** IA practitioners following the Rosenfeld/Morville "Information Architecture for the Web" tradition treat card sorting and controlled vocabularies as the standard method for reconciling organizational taxonomies with user mental models before committing to a site's top-level navigation labels; the same tradition emphasizes that a single "correct" taxonomy rarely exists for complex domains, and that cross-referencing (the same item reachable from multiple paths) is a deliberate IA technique rather than a failure state.
- **[Industry Best Practice]** In healthcare/eldercare retail (e.g., general observation of sites like Medline's "Home Essentials," ElderStore, and Carex home-care collections), category structures observed in the wild tend to mix condition-based groupings ("Incontinence," "Arthritis," "Fall Prevention") with product-type groupings ("Mobility," "Bathroom Safety") rather than picking one taxonomy exclusively — an implicit acknowledgment that no single organizing principle satisfies all caregivers.

## Important Findings
- **[Observation]** EnconeMed's candidate labels split cleanly into two different cognitive levels: "Equipment," "Accessories," and "Care Essentials" are *object-type* superordinate categories (closer to how a warehouse or a clinician would sort inventory), while "Home ICU," "Respiratory," "Mobility," "Recovery," and "Patient Care" are *situation/need-based* categories (closer to how a caregiver experiencing a specific life event would think — "my mother just had hip surgery," "my father is on oxygen now").
- **[Observation]** This is not a contradiction to resolve by picking one — it mirrors the well-documented tension in card-sort research between organizational logic and user logic. The practical implication is that EnconeMed likely needs *both* an object-type taxonomy (useful for someone who already knows they need "a hospital bed") and a situation-based taxonomy (useful for someone who does not yet know that "a hospital bed" is the object they need), with cross-linking between them.
- **[Recommendation]** Treat "Home ICU," "Respiratory," "Mobility," "Recovery," and "Patient Care" as **basic-level, situation-anchored entry points** for the stressed layperson — these map to how a first-time caregiver actually thinks ("Dad just came home from the hospital and needs oxygen" → Respiratory), not to how a clinician would classify the underlying device (e.g., an oxygen concentrator is clinically "respiratory therapy DME," but to the caregiver it is simply "the breathing machine"). This grounding is a direct application of Rosch's basic-level principle: the caregiver reaches for the level of abstraction that requires the least prior domain knowledge to be useful.
- **[Recommendation]** Treat "Equipment," "Accessories," and "Care Essentials" as **superordinate, object-type categories** useful primarily for repeat/informed users (a returning customer, a discharge planner, a nurse) who already know the object class they want and are optimizing for speed, not discovery. This matches the clinician/expert mental model, which tends to organize by product taxonomy and specification rather than by life situation.
- **[Recommendation]** Do not force a single hierarchy where situation-based categories are nested *under* object-type categories or vice versa. Card-sort literature and Rosch's basic-level research both suggest that forcing an expert taxonomy onto a novice user (or forcing a symptom-based taxonomy onto a professional buyer) increases search cost. A **flat, cross-linked structure** — where, for example, an oxygen concentrator page is reachable from both "Respiratory" and "Equipment > Oxygen Therapy" — better serves both audiences.
- **[Observation]** "Care Essentials" (consumables: gloves, masks, diapers, catheters, urine bags, feeding tubes) is cognitively distinct from "Equipment" even though both might sit under a single superordinate "products" idea internally — consumables are recurring, low-consideration, non-durable purchases, while equipment is a considered, often rental, durable-goods decision. This distinction is explored further in `18-care-essentials-research.md`, but the IA implication here is that consumables likely deserve their own top-level cognitive slot rather than being a sub-category of "Equipment" or "Accessories," since combining them may make Care Essentials effectively invisible to someone whose basic-level mental model separates "the machine" from "the supplies that get used up."
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Because card-sort research explicitly warns that participants keyword-match against imprecise labels, generic or clinical labels ("Durable Medical Equipment," "Ambulatory Assistive Devices") risk being misread or skipped over by non-expert caregivers relative to plain-language, basic-level labels ("Mobility," "Wheelchairs & Walkers").

## Design Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Run (or budget for) a moderated or unmoderated card sort with a realistic Indian caregiver sample (not clinicians) using plain-language item cards (e.g., "the machine that helps Dad breathe," "the bed that raises up") rather than product SKUs, to validate which of the candidate top-level labels group intuitively before committing to final IA in a later phase.
- **[Recommendation]** Provide at least two parallel "doors" into the catalog: a situation/need-based door (Home ICU, Respiratory, Mobility, Recovery, Patient Care) for first-time, high-stress caregivers, and an object-type door (Equipment, Accessories, Care Essentials) for repeat or professional buyers — both should be visible in primary navigation rather than one being buried as a sub-path of the other.
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid deep nesting of situation categories under object categories (e.g., do not bury "Respiratory" three levels beneath "Equipment > Therapy Devices > Respiratory"), since this reintroduces the clinician's hierarchy on top of the layperson's entry point and defeats the purpose of having a situation-based door at all.
- **[Observation]** Labels chosen for situation-based categories should be tested for basic-level fit specifically with the *target* caregiver demographic (likely adult children arranging care for aging parents, per the health-literacy research above), since basic-level categories are not universal — they shift with the categorizer's domain expertise.

## Business Implications
- **[Recommendation]** A dual-taxonomy IA (situation-based + object-based) is more expensive to build and maintain (more cross-links, more landing pages, more content ownership) than a single taxonomy, but the alternative — forcing one mental model on both audiences — risks losing either the high-intent professional/repeat buyer (if the site is all symptom-quiz style) or the panicked first-time caregiver (if the site reads like a clinical supply catalog). Budget and content-ops planning in later phases should account for maintaining both entry structures.
- **[Observation]** Because "Care Essentials" consumables likely represent a different (recurring, lower-margin-per-unit but higher-frequency) revenue model than "Equipment" rental/sale, keeping it as a cognitively distinct top-level entity also supports a distinct business/marketing treatment (e.g., reorder flows, subscription mechanics — see file 18) rather than diluting it inside a general equipment catalog.

## SEO Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Situation-based category pages (Home ICU, Respiratory, Mobility, Recovery, Patient Care) are likely to align well with how non-expert users phrase search queries ("oxygen concentrator for home use after hospital," "bed for bedridden patient at home") and should be built as substantive, keyword-rich landing pages rather than thin category stubs, since they mirror natural-language, symptom/situation-based search intent documented in the health-information-seeking literature above (evidence-based and hypothesis-based search patterns).
- **[Observation]** Object-type category pages (Equipment > Oxygen Concentrators, etc.) are likely to capture more precise, higher-purchase-intent, product-name queries from users who already know what they want — both category types are complementary for SEO coverage rather than redundant.

## AI Search Implications
- **[Recommendation]** AI answer engines (e.g., conversational search / LLM-based assistants) are likely to paraphrase user needs in situation/symptom language ("what equipment does someone need after a stroke at home") more often than in precise product taxonomy language; maintaining well-structured situation-based category content (with clear headings, FAQ-style content, and structured data) improves the odds of being surfaced as a citation or answer source in AI search contexts, consistent with the basic-level, need-first framing recommended above.
- **[Observation]** Because AI search summarization tends to compress category hierarchies, a flatter, well-labeled IA (fewer forced nesting levels) is more likely to be represented accurately when an AI system summarizes "what EnconeMed offers" than a deep clinical taxonomy would be.

## Recommendations
- **[Recommendation]** Adopt a dual-door IA: situation-based categories (Home ICU, Respiratory, Mobility, Recovery, Patient Care) as the primary, basic-level entry point for first-time caregivers, and object-type categories (Equipment, Accessories, Care Essentials) as a secondary, expert-oriented entry point — cross-linked, not nested.
- **[Recommendation]** Validate top-level labels with a lightweight card sort or tree test using realistic caregiver-language item cards before locking the IA in a later design phase.
- **[Recommendation]** Keep "Care Essentials" as its own top-level cognitive category distinct from "Equipment," reflecting the durable-vs-consumable psychological distinction documented in file 18.
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid over-clinical labels; prefer basic-level plain language validated against the target caregiver demographic's actual vocabulary.

## Things To Avoid
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid designing the IA solely around internal/operational logic (e.g., how the warehouse or supplier catalog is organized) — this is the single most-cited failure mode in card-sorting literature.
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid forcing users to know a clinical or product-taxonomy term before they can find what they need (e.g., requiring the user to know "concentrator" rather than allowing entry via "oxygen" or "breathing").
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid collapsing situation-based and object-based categories into a single hierarchy where one is subordinate to the other; this reintroduces a single mental model where two are needed.
- **[Observation]** Avoid assuming the IA validated by this research is final — this document reasons from general cognitive-science and UX-methodology literature, not from a card sort conducted specifically with EnconeMed's target Indian caregiver population; treat the groupings above as testable hypotheses, not settled conclusions.

## Future Considerations
- **[Recommendation]** Commission an actual moderated card sort and/or tree test with representative Indian caregivers (and, separately, with clinicians/discharge planners) once the product catalog is more finalized, to empirically validate or revise the dual-door IA hypothesis proposed here.
- **[Observation]** As EnconeMed's catalog grows (more categories, more consumable SKUs), periodic re-validation of basic-level fit is advisable, since basic-level categorization can shift as a user population's domain familiarity grows (e.g., a caregiver managing a parent's condition for two years may develop a more clinical vocabulary than a first-week caregiver).

## Checklist
- [ ] Distinguish situation-based IA doors from object-type IA doors in all future navigation/IA design work.
- [ ] Do not nest one taxonomy inside the other; use cross-linking instead.
- [ ] Validate top-level category labels against real caregiver vocabulary (not clinical vocabulary) before finalizing.
- [ ] Keep Care Essentials cognitively and structurally separate from Equipment.
- [ ] Plan for a future card sort / tree test with actual target users as a validation step, not a replacement for this desk research.
- [ ] Ensure situation-based category pages are built as substantive SEO/AI-search-friendly landing pages, not thin stubs.
