# Product Discovery Research — Finding Products Without Knowing Exact Names

## Executive Summary
This document researches how users discover products when they don't know the exact product name — the browsing-vs-searching split documented by Baymard Institute, which filters/facets matter most for a medical-equipment catalog (use-case/condition-based facets vs. spec-based facets), and the documented role of comparison tables. The headline finding is that the browse/search split is not fixed — it depends on catalog type and device. On general e-commerce sites Baymard found roughly a 50-50 split between searchers and browsers, but on mobile, search is generally preferred because it feels faster, and on sites with flatter, simpler hierarchies (like apparel), the large majority of users default to browsing. For medical equipment specifically — a domain where buyers often do not know product names at all — this suggests category/situation-based browsing must be a first-class discovery path, not a fallback to search. On filters, Baymard's research shows only 16% of e-commerce sites offer an effective faceted-search experience, and that quality/relevance of filters matters more than quantity — directly relevant to deciding between clinical/spec facets (e.g., "weight capacity," "oxygen output in LPM") and use-case facets (e.g., "for bedridden patients," "for outdoor use"). On comparison tables, Baymard found that in spec-driven verticals 67% of users engage comparison features when available, yet 17% of spec-driven sites still don't offer one — a clear and directly transferable finding for a specification-heavy category like medical equipment.

## Research Scope
- Baymard's browsing-vs-searching research and how it varies by catalog type and device.
- Facet/filter design research, applied to the distinction between use-case-based and spec-based facets for medical equipment.
- Comparison table research for spec-driven product categories.
- Out of scope: cognitive/IA categorization theory (file 05), navigation chrome like mega-menus and breadcrumbs (file 06), guided-selling quiz tools (file 17).

## Evidence
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Baymard's research states: "Search is typically preferred when users generally know the exact item they are looking for, while category browsing tends to be preferred when most users only know the type of product they are looking for" (Baymard Institute, "Category Navigation & Curated Paths Vs Search," https://baymard.com/blog/apparel-search).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** On apparel and accessories sites, Baymard found the initial product-finding behavior of 100% of test participants was to principally use the main navigation (and to a lesser degree the homepage) to browse, because these catalogs tend to have flatter hierarchies and fewer products (Baymard Institute, "Category Navigation & Curated Paths Vs Search," https://baymard.com/blog/apparel-search).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** On general e-commerce sites (Amazon, Walmart, Best Buy, etc.), Baymard's research found a roughly 50-50 split between users who prefer to search vs. users who prefer to browse via main navigation (Baymard Institute, "2024 Product Finding Research Update," https://baymard.com/blog/product-finding-2024-launch).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** On mobile specifically, search was generally the preferred product-finding strategy among Baymard's test subjects, perceived as faster than category navigation on a small screen (Baymard Institute, "The State of Mobile E-Commerce Search and Category Navigation," https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-ecommerce-search-and-navigation).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Baymard's e-commerce filtering research finds that at a minimum, most sites should offer filters like Price, Brand, Average Rating, Size/Color as applicable, plus relevant category-specific attributes; the most useful filters vary meaningfully by product category (e.g., electronics buyers prioritize Brand/Price/Features, fashion buyers prioritize Size/Color/Fit) (Baymard Institute, "What Is an Ecommerce Filter? UI Best Practices," https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-filter-ui).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Baymard found that users benefit from more *relevant* filters but struggle when filter groups get too long, aren't prioritized, or hide the impact of a selection — i.e., filter quality and relevance outperform filter quantity (Baymard Institute, cross-referenced summary via "What Is an Ecommerce Filter? UI Best Practices," https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-filter-ui).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** Baymard Institute research found that only 16% of e-commerce sites offer what it considers an effective faceted-search/filtering experience, and that 57% of sites have poor filtering experiences that contribute to cart abandonment (Baymard Institute, cross-referenced via "What Is an Ecommerce Filter? UI Best Practices," https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-filter-ui).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** For "spec-driven industries," Baymard found 67% of test participants used comparison features when available during testing, yet 17% of sites in spec-driven categories still fail to offer any comparison tool at all — described as a clear usability gap given documented demand (Baymard Institute, "Product Comparison UX: Always Provide Comparison Features for Spec-Driven Industries," https://baymard.com/blog/provide-comparison-features).
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** A well-implemented comparison feature, per Baymard, allows users to see every relevant spec for up to five products at once, and is specifically valuable because visiting multiple product pages one-by-one to manually compare specs is time-consuming and causes users to hesitate or abandon comparison altogether (Baymard Institute, "4 Ways to Optimize the Comparison Feature for Scanning," https://baymard.com/blog/user-friendly-comparison-tools").
- **[Peer-Reviewed/Research-Backed]** More than 50% of users, per Baymard's mobile research, attempt to "search within" their currently browsed category — treating the search bar as an in-context filter rather than a full catalog reset — and 94% of mobile sites fail to support this (Baymard Institute, "Mobile Usability: Allow Users to 'Search Within' Their Current Category," https://baymard.com/blog/search-within-current-category).

## Industry Research
- **[Industry Best Practice]** In the medical/home-healthcare-equipment retail space generally, observed category structures (e.g., Medline, Carex, Walgreens home medical) tend to expose both condition/use-case groupings and spec-oriented filters side by side, suggesting that in this specific vertical, neither browsing nor searching alone is sufficient — a caregiver's initial entry is typically situational ("post-surgery," "incontinence") but the final purchase decision is typically spec-driven ("weight capacity 300 lbs," "bariatric," "electric vs. manual").
- **[Industry Best Practice]** Specification lists on product pages alone (without an aggregated comparison view) are documented elsewhere as having poor usability for side-by-side decision-making, reinforcing Baymard's comparison-tool findings (secondary UX literature, e.g. "Specification Lists Have Terrible Usability," UX Tigers, https://www.uxtigers.com/post/specification-lists — note: this is a single-author blog synthesis, not a peer-reviewed Baymard/NN/g study, and should be weighted as directional industry commentary only).

## Important Findings
- **[Observation]** Medical equipment is a domain where a large share of first-time buyers/renters likely do not know the exact product name at all (a discharge planner or nurse says "you'll need a hospital bed and a wheelchair" but the caregiver has never shopped for either) — this pushes EnconeMed's catalog toward the "browsing-dominant" end of Baymard's spectrum (similar to apparel's flatter-hierarchy pattern) for first-time/first-purchase users, while repeat buyers and professional buyers (clinics, agencies) are more likely to fall into the "search-dominant" pattern of knowing exact SKUs or model names.
- **[Observation]** This dual pattern reinforces the file 05 recommendation for a dual-taxonomy IA: situation-based browsing paths serve the "don't know the name" buyer, while search (ideally with terminology-bridging autocomplete per file 06) serves the "know the name" buyer.
- **[Observation]** For medical equipment specifically, facets naturally split into two families: **use-case/condition facets** (e.g., "for bedridden patients," "for outdoor/all-terrain use," "for post-surgical recovery," "bariatric/heavy-duty") and **spec facets** (e.g., weight capacity, oxygen output in LPM, manual vs. electric, foldable vs. fixed, battery life). Baymard's research that filter *relevance* matters more than filter *quantity* suggests EnconeMed should not expose an undifferentiated flat list of every spec; instead, use-case facets likely belong "above the fold" as primary filters, with spec facets as secondary/advanced filters — mirroring how a caregiver's decision process typically moves from situation to specification, not the reverse.
- **[Observation]** Given documented findings that comparison tools see high engagement (67%) in spec-driven categories, and medical equipment is unambiguously spec-driven (capacity, dimensions, certifications, rental vs. purchase price), a comparison table capability is very likely to be a high-value feature for EnconeMed rather than a nice-to-have.

## Design Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Support both discovery paths as first-class experiences: browsing via situation-based categories for buyers who don't know product names, and search (with terminology-bridging autocomplete) for buyers who do.
- **[Recommendation]** Structure filters in two tiers: primary/default-visible use-case facets (condition, care setting, patient mobility level) and secondary/expandable spec facets (dimensions, capacity, power source, certifications) — consistent with Baymard's finding that filter relevance and prioritization outweigh sheer filter count.
- **[Recommendation]** Build a comparison table feature for spec-heavy categories (hospital beds, wheelchairs, oxygen concentrators, patient monitors) allowing side-by-side comparison of up to ~5 products, following Baymard's documented pattern for spec-driven verticals.
- **[Recommendation]** Support "search within category" so a caregiver already browsing "Mobility" who searches "lightweight" gets scoped results, not a full-catalog reset.

## Business Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Because rental vs. purchase pricing, deposit terms, and delivery timelines are themselves spec-like decision factors for medical equipment, consider surfacing them as comparison-table rows/columns, not just physical specs — this is a domain-specific extension of Baymard's spec-driven comparison finding and could differentiate EnconeMed from competitors who only compare physical specs.
- **[Observation]** Investing in a genuinely effective faceted search experience is a differentiator given Baymard's finding that only 16% of e-commerce sites achieve this — likely true or worse in the Indian medical-equipment vertical specifically, where competitor sites are unlikely to have invested in filter UX research.

## SEO Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Use-case facet values (e.g., "wheelchairs for bariatric patients," "beds for post-surgical recovery") often mirror real long-tail search queries and should be exposed as crawlable, filterable landing pages (not JS-only filter states) where feasible, to capture long-tail organic search demand aligned with how non-expert buyers phrase queries.
- **[Observation]** Comparison pages/tables, if built as indexable content (not purely session-based interactive widgets), can themselves become valuable SEO landing pages for "X vs Y" style queries common in considered-purchase categories like medical equipment.

## AI Search Implications
- **[Recommendation]** Structured, crawlable comparison tables and clearly labeled use-case facets are more likely to be accurately summarized or cited by AI answer engines responding to comparative or need-based queries ("best oxygen concentrator for home use," "hospital bed vs. adjustable bed for elderly") than product pages relying solely on unstructured prose specs.
- **[Observation]** Because AI search tools often synthesize an answer from multiple structured facts rather than a single page, ensuring specs and use-case attributes are marked up consistently (e.g., structured data, consistent attribute naming) across the catalog likely improves the odds of accurate AI-generated summaries about EnconeMed's offerings.

## Recommendations
- **[Recommendation]** Support both browsing and search as co-equal discovery paths, not search-as-fallback.
- **[Recommendation]** Prioritize use-case facets over spec facets in default filter visibility; keep specs available but secondary.
- **[Recommendation]** Build a side-by-side comparison table for spec-driven categories, including rental/purchase commercial terms as comparable attributes.
- **[Recommendation]** Support in-category search scoping.

## Things To Avoid
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid exposing a long, undifferentiated, alphabetically-sorted filter list — Baymard's research explicitly warns this underperforms a smaller, prioritized, relevance-ranked filter set.
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid treating search as the sole or primary discovery mechanism given how many first-time medical-equipment buyers do not know exact product names.
- **[Recommendation]** Avoid omitting a comparison tool for spec-heavy categories — Baymard's research frames this as a clear, well-documented gap rather than a matter of taste.

## Future Considerations
- **[Recommendation]** Once real catalog data and facet values exist, conduct a lightweight usability test (even 5-6 participants) specifically on filter comprehension for the use-case-facet vocabulary chosen, since Baymard's general filter research is not medical-equipment-specific and EnconeMed's use-case labels will need independent validation.
- **[Observation]** As order history accumulates, consider whether search-vs-browse behavior split for EnconeMed specifically matches Baymard's general 50-50 or apparel-like patterns — this is an empirical question that can be answered with real analytics once the site is live, not assumed from this desk research.
